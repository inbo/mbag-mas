# English abstract {-}
This report evaluates the statistical feasibility of a simplified monitoring design for monitoring birds in agricultural areas in Flanders, referred to as "MAS light". The objective of this monitoring scheme is to assess whether population trends of farmland birds within Species Protection Programme (SPP) areas are more favourable than those in comparable areas outside SPP areas. The central question is what minimum difference in population trend between these two groups can be detected with sufficient statistical confidence.

To address this question, a simulation-based power analysis was conducted. Count data were generated using a Poisson generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) and subsequently analysed using the same model. The minimum detectable effect (MDE) was estimated for scenarios with 100, 200, and 400 survey points for a time series of 10, 16, and 24 years. The effect size was defined as the smallest detectable difference in population trend between SPP and non-SPP areas.

The simulations show that the detectable effect size decreases as both the number of survey points ($n$) and the length of the time series ($T$) increase, and is approximately proportional to $1/\sqrt{nT^3}$. The analyses were performed for a hypothetical optimal farmland bird species with high population density and limited variation among survey points. Consequently, the results represent an optimistic estimate of the minimum detectable effect size; for rarer species or species with a more heterogeneous distribution, the minimum detectable effect will be larger.

Even for this hypothetical optimal farmland bird species, the minimum detectable difference in population trend remains relatively large. In a monitoring scheme with 400 survey points, only around 200 survey points may, in practice, provide informative observations for a realistic species that does not occur everywhere. Under these conditions, only a substantially more favourable trend within SPP areas than outside SPP areas can be detected. Specifically, over a 10-year period, the population within SPP areas would need to show a trend approximately 60 % more favourable than that outside SPP areas before a statistically significant difference could be demonstrated. For example, if the population declines by 9.5 % outside SPP areas over 10 years, it would need to increase by 46.2 % within SPP areas. If the population remains stable outside SPP areas, it would need to increase by 58.4 % within SPP areas, while an increase of 10.5 % outside SPP areas would require an increase of 73.3 % within SPP areas to yield a statistically significant difference. For a 24-year time series, the required difference is smaller: the population within SPP areas would need to exhibit a trend that is 37.6 % more favourable than that outside SPP areas before a statistically significant difference could be detected.

These results indicate that reducing the number of survey points is not advisable, as doing so would substantially reduce the statistical power of the monitoring scheme and limit its ability to reliably detect differences in population trends between SPP and non-SPP areas.
